# August 10: optimal stirling engine crankshaft linkage

Motiongen is a great tool to experiment linkages. I learned that stirling engines should have a phase difference of 90 degrees. In other words, the displacer and power piston positions should be offset by 90 degrees, with the displacer being ahead. Using linkage simulations like Motiongen made this clearer for me! At first, I made a couple of small mistakes, such as putting the power piston ahead, switching their positions, etc. I found the importance of prototyping and experimenting.

# Sept 5th: Building the first stirling engine


Today I assembled the printed parts together to see how everything fit!
While the rhombic drive moved alright, there were some flaws that could be fixed. For example, there was too much friction, I learned, because the axles of the gears were directly rubbing against the edge of the 3d-printed gear holder. Additionally, when the linkages from the rhombic drive were closest together, they would sag a little. I found that the rhombic drive relied on tight geometry----my CAD was not restrictive enough, allowing for play in the linkage.

# Sept 12th: Modeling bearings

During my research, I found that bearings were a great way to reduce friction in rotation! It's interesting how many different types of 3d printed print-in-place bearings there are. I decided to use the dumbbell configuration (instead of ball or roller), because it was easier to print and reduced friction. It was a game of testing tolerances, sizes, and revolving in Onshape. Lots of revolving.

# Sept 19th: Fixing the rhombic drive

I fixed the CAD geometry for the rhombic drive! Apparently, making the linkages more geometrically constrictive actually meant making the links a little longer. This allows the drive to stay in a non-sagging position when the points on the gears are closest together. However, the gears now need to be bigger/adjusted accordingly. Now I reprinted and rebuilt the linkage. This happened about 2-3 times, as I gradually increased the length of the linkages until they no longer sagged.

# Sept 26th: Using bearings

I printed the bearings! The problem with the bearings was that they were a little too tight, making them hard to spin. At the same time, I didn't want the dumbbells inside to fall out of the cage. I learned that adjusting the gap between the dumbbells and the cage, alongside trial and error, allowed me to find the optimal bearing print. The next issue was adding the bearing into the design. Initially I thought of having the gears spin around a bearing, but in the occurrence of the gear and flywheel on the same axle, that wouldn't work, because they would spin independently. For some reason, I was really stuck on this issue for a while until I finally realized that the bearing could be fixed and the axle spun inside the bearing. 
Another issue was radial play misaligning the bearing. Putting plates on both sides of the bearing allowed the play to be limited. However, the addition of bearings really complicated the design and its assembly. As I built the physical thing, I had to consider ease of assembly and how many screws I needed.

# Oct 3rd: Assembling the stirling engine (again)

After assembling the linkage, I could finally attach the pistons to the linkages. 
I had to split the displacer to make it possible to insert it through the power piston, while still being able to connect to the linkages. The result was 3 pieces for the displacer; thankfully the power piston was able to stay whole. The gears no longer wobbled when spun thanks to the play limiters, and the linkage seemed perfectly symmetric. The whole Stirling engine was finally assembled.

# October 10th: Reducing friction
There was a big issue with friction. While the bearing worked well now after the adjustment, I couldn't help but try to further reduce friction. However, what I had achieved previously was actually the best. The rest of my prints and attempts were weak or about the same quality. I learned that the core issue was with the o-ring on the power piston and the inconsistencies with the piston rod prints. The solution was to remove the o-ring, since it had no use (the plastic would melt under the fire that would be needed to power the engine), and to sand down the piston rod. The beta engine model finally had a moderately acceptable amount of friction!
